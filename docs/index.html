what is vibe coding, actually

It's steering, not typing.

You describe what you want in plain English. An AI writes the code. Then the interesting part starts — you run it, decide whether it's actually right, and tell it what to change. Over and over, until it's genuinely good.

01

Ask

"Build a dice roller with a big ROLL button that shows a number from 1 to 20 in huge text." That's a real prompt — and knowing what to say is the whole skill.

02

Run

A window opens on your screen. Sometimes it's exactly right. Sometimes it crashes. Sometimes it works perfectly and does completely the wrong thing.

03

Fix

You say what broke: what you did, what you expected, what actually happened. Turns out that's how you report a bug to any human engineer too. Convenient.

the part nobody tells you

It ran ≠ it's right.

The AI is a spectacularly fast guesser. It has read more code than any human ever will, and it will confidently hand you something broken with a completely straight face.

It's like a genius intern on day one. Incredibly fast, knows every library, has no idea what you actually want — and will never tell you it's confused.

Catching that is the actual class. Anyone can get code to run. Not everyone can tell whether it's correct — and that skill doesn't expire when the tools change.

# asked for: a quiz that shows my score for q in questions: ans = input(q["text"]) if ans == q["correct"]: score += 1 print(f"That's {score/len(questions)*100}%!")

Nothing on your computer will ever flag this. That's your job — and by session two you'll spot it in about four seconds.

the arc

Four sessions that build on each other.

Session 1 stands completely on its own — if you can only make one, you'll still leave with a finished app you built.

01

Make Something Work

No lecture first. Your instructor builds an app live, then you build one, then you break it on purpose and fix what you broke.

02

Ask Better, Check Harder

How much to ask for at once, and how to actually verify what came back. Ends with everyone swapping computers to try to break a partner's app.

03

Make It Solid / Build Something Bigger

The two tracks genuinely split here — not just pacing, different material.

MSSave points, debugging technique, and hardening one app until it refuses to break.
HSMulti-file projects, planning before building, and finishing the capstone.
04

Make It Yours / Demo Day

Everyone demos. Two things are required every time: one thing that broke, and one thing the AI got wrong that you caught. Nobody gets to look like it was effortless.

questions you probably have

Reasonable concerns, addressed.

Do I need to know how to code?

No. Genuinely, actually no — not "no, but it helps if you do." Campers who have never written a line of code build a working app in their first session. If you do already code, you'll get more out of it, because you'll catch things other people miss.

Isn't this just letting a robot do my homework?

Fair question, and we're upfront about it in class. The AI writes the code. You decide what gets built, catch what's wrong, and steer the whole thing — which is most of what professional engineers actually spend their time doing. We also tell you plainly what you haven't learned yet, and exactly how to go learn it.

What do I need to bring?

Nothing. Lab laptops are set up and ready — no installs, no accounts, no software to download. Show up and open a terminal.

What if I miss a session?

Session 1 is deliberately built to stand alone, so you can attend only that one and still leave with a finished app. Later sessions each open with a way to catch newcomers up.

What language do we use?

Python, with a library called tkinter that makes windows and buttons. It was picked because there's nothing to install and you get a real window on screen in about sixty seconds. Already fluent in something else? Talk to your instructor.

Do I get to keep what I build?

Yes. It's yours — email it to yourself or bring a USB stick. The tools are free to install at home, so you can keep going the day after camp ends.
